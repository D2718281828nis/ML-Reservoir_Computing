Training Only the Readout: Reservoir Computing versus Feed-Forward Networks

There are two classic ways to forecast a time series with a neural network. You can hand a network a window of the last values and train every weight with backpropagation: a feed-forward network. Or you can let the series drive a large recurrent network that is random and never trained, and fit only a linear readout of its state, in one least-squares solve: reservoir computing. This page builds both in your browser, on the same data, and measures what the difference buys and what it costs.

1

Two Approaches to Learning from Past Observations

Both families answer the same question: given the values so far, what comes next? They disagree on two things: where the network keeps its memory of the past, and which of its weights are learned.

1.1 Problem Formulation and Two Model Classes

FFN
window z(t)…z(t−L+1)L numbers→ hidden layerstanhtrained→ outputtrained→ forecast ŷ(t+h)
RC
input u(t)1 number per step→ W_inrandom, fixed→ reservoir x(t)N numbersrandom, fixed→ readout W_outtrained→ forecast ŷ(t+h)
FFN: ŷ(t+h) = f_θ(window), θ trained by gradient descent
ESN: x(t) = (1−α) x(t−1) + α tanh(W_in u(t) + W x(t−1) + b), ŷ = W_out [1; u; x]

In the FFN every weight moves during training. The loss is non-convex, so training takes many small gradient steps, and different seeds end in different places. In the reservoir, the echo state network (ESN) of Jaeger (2001), W_in and W are drawn at random once and frozen. The state x(t) becomes a long, nonlinear, fading summary of everything the input has done. The readout is a linear regression on that state: a convex problem with one exact solution.

Notation in the ESN equation: α ∈ (0, 1] is the leak rate, the fraction of the new value blended into the state at each step. With α = 1 the state is replaced entirely; with small α it changes slowly and smoothly. ρ is the spectral radius of W, the largest modulus of its eigenvalues. It does not appear in the equation explicitly: after W is drawn at random, it is rescaled so that ρ takes the chosen value. The closer ρ is to 1, the longer a disturbance circulates through the recurrent links and the longer the reservoir's memory. Section 1.3 illustrates both; part 4 examines their effect on memory and stability.

1.2 Two Independent Design Choices

"Feed-forward vs reservoir" bundles two separate choices. One is where the memory lives: in an explicit window of past values, or in the state of a recurrent system. The other is what is trained: every weight, or only the last linear layer. All four combinations are real models, and the page builds three of them.

every weight trained (backprop) only the readout trained (one solve) window FFN / MLPwindow → trained hidden layers → output · part 3 ELM NG-RCwindow → fixed random or polynomial features → readout · parts 5, 7 state RNN, LSTM, GRUrecurrent, trained through time · not built here; see notebooks 01 and 02 ESN, LSMrecurrent, random and fixed → readout · part 4

The two distinctions are best treated separately, since they affect different properties of a model. Part 5 compares the models occupying the cells of this table on a common task. The ELM (extreme learning machine: a window of inputs mapped through a fixed random hidden layer) differs from the FFN only in which parameters are trained, and from the ESN only in how memory is represented. The comparison therefore isolates the contribution of each factor.

1.3 Reservoir Hyperparameters: The Leak Rate α and the Spectral Radius ρ

Two numbers that are set in advance, not trained, govern the dynamics of an ESN.

  • The leak rate α ∈ (0, 1] is the fraction of the new value blended into the state at each step. With α = 1 the state is replaced entirely; with small α it changes slowly and smoothly, and each neuron acts as a low-pass filter with time constant τ = −1/ln(1−α) ≈ 1/α steps.
  • The spectral radius ρ(W) = max |λᵢ(W)| is the largest modulus of the eigenvalues of W. It does not appear in the state equation explicitly: after the matrix W₀ is drawn at random, it is rescaled so that ρ takes the chosen value. Eigenvalues scale linearly with the matrix, so after multiplication by ρ/ρ₀ the largest modulus is exactly ρ.
x(t) = (1−α) x(t−1) + α d(t)
W = (ρ/ρ₀) W₀, ρ₀ = max |λ(W₀)|

The left panel isolates the leak: a given signal d(t) with level jumps and noise replaces tanh(·) in the equation. With α = 1 the state follows the signal, noise included; with α ≈ 0.1 the noise is smoothed out, but the state lags the jumps by about 10 steps. The right panel shows the eigenvalues of one random matrix for 100 neurons. The ρ slider stretches or shrinks the whole cloud without changing its shape. The chart beside the plane is the size of a disturbance carried by the recurrent links alone, without the nonlinearity or the input. Its slope on the log scale tends to log ρ, so ρ sets how fast the reservoir forgets the past: the closer ρ is to 1, the longer the memory (part 4.3).

The Forward Pass of a Single Forecast in Both Networks

Before taking the two models apart, watch them work. Both were trained on the same data (Mackey–Glass, the value 20 steps ahead; parts 3–5 explain how). The view below follows one time step from the input to both forecasts, the way Transformer Explainer follows a sentence through GPT-2: one continuous flow, bands as wide as the vectors they carry, and every part open to inspection with this step's numbers.

2.1 Experiment: A Single Time Step from Input to Output

Each column is a vector, drawn as a strip of numbers (blue negative, red positive) at 3 pixels per number, so a band is as wide as the vector it carries and a matrix that changes a vector's length becomes a funnel. Dashed means random and fixed; solid means trained. Step or Play through the test period, hover a strip for its exact value, and click a column or a magnifier to open its math. New here? Take the guided tour.

2.2 Unrolling the Recurrence in Time: From x(t) to x(t−1)

The arrow under the reservoir in 2.1 is the whole trick of a recurrent network, so here it is unrolled in time. The four columns are the same 100 neurons at four consecutive steps. Each update reads one column and the new input and writes the next column:

x(t) = (1−α) x(t−1) + α tanh(W_in u(t) + W x(t−1) + b)

"x(t) becomes x(t−1)" means nothing more than this: the column written at step t is the column read at step t + 1. Press Step and watch. The slot names stay where they are, and the columns slide left under them, so the strip you just saw as x(t) is now labeled x(t−1). Nothing is copied and nothing else is stored: there is no window and no buffer of old inputs. Whatever the ESN can know about u(t−10) has survived ten passes through this update, along two paths. The leak keeps a fraction 1 − α of each neuron's own value (the straight band). W mixes in the 10 neurons each neuron listens to (the curved lines, drawn for the neuron you follow, colored and sized by what each passes on).

2.3 Graph Representation of the Reservoir

The same 100 neurons, drawn as what they are: a directed graph. Each neuron listens to 10 others, which makes 1,000 links, with random weights scaled to the spectral radius ρ. Hover a neuron to see whom it listens to and who listens to it; click it to follow it in 2.1 and 2.2. The time step, the selected neuron and the ρ, α and λ settings are shared with 2.1. To the left of the graph is the input node u(t), linked to every neuron through the fixed matrix W_in. To the right is the forecast node ŷ(t+h), into which all 100 neurons feed through the trained readout W_out. The graph itself (the matrix W) sits between them, as the reservoir does in the diagram of 2.1.

Choose echo of one change and press Play. Nudging one neuron's state by 0.5 shows how information travels along the links. At k = 0 only that neuron differs; one step later, the dozen or so that listen to it; two steps later, about 70 of the 100; and then the difference fades step by step. That fading is the echo state property of part 4.2, seen from the inside. Changing one input value instead reaches every neuron at once, because W_in feeds all of them, and its effect on the forecast dies out over about 40 steps: that is how long this reservoir remembers a single value.

A second look at the readout. With the λ chosen on validation, a nudge of 0.5 to a single neuron moves the forecast by about 6 standard deviations (the median over neurons; more than 50 for some). A real input never pushes the state off its usual track like that, so the test error stays low. But it is the large, cancelling readout weights of 2.1 at work. Set λ = 10⁻⁴ in 2.1 and the same nudge moves the forecast by about 0.2.

2.4 Discussion

  • Width is memory. The FFN's input band is 20 numbers wide, and nothing comes back from its hidden layer: at every step it starts from scratch. The ESN's input is a thread, a single number, but a 100-number band arrives from the previous step. The state carries the past.
  • Dashed is free. In the FFN every band is solid: W₁ and W₂, 353 weights, were trained by backpropagation. In the ESN only the last band is. W_in and W were drawn at random; the 102 weights of W_out were fitted in one ridge solve.
  • The readout is a distributed code. Open the ESN readout. The forecast is a sum of 102 terms, and no dozen neurons carry it. At the λ chosen on validation the terms reach ±50 and cancel almost exactly; that precision buys the low error. Switch λ to 10⁻⁴ and the weights shrink about fiftyfold and become readable, while the test error grows from about 0.004 to about 0.05.
  • Change the reservoir, keep the training. Move ρ or α. The strips change character (slow and smooth at small α), the readout is re-fitted at once, and the ESN's test error moves. The FFN cannot follow without another round of training.

This view is modeled on Transformer Explainer (A. Cho, G. C. Kim, A. Karpekov, A. Helbling, J. Wang, S. Lee, B. Hoover, D. H. Chau, CHI 2026), which shows GPT-2's data flow the same way. Its four design goals carry over: a continuous overview of the data flow, expanded views that reveal the math with live values, live experimentation, and a model that runs in the browser.

Feed-Forward Networks as Functions of a Finite Window

A feed-forward network has no memory of its own. Everything it knows about the past is in its input window, and the window length L is a decision you make before training starts.

3.1 Model Definition

w = window, h₁ = tanh(W₁w + b₁), h₂ = tanh(W₂h₁ + b₂), ŷ = W₃h₂ + b₃

Training is mini-batch gradient descent with Adam on the mean squared error. Backpropagation computes how the loss depends on every weight, and every weight takes a small step. With L = 20 and two layers of 32 units that is 1,761 weights, all learned.

The data for parts 3–5 is the Mackey–Glass series, a classic benchmark for chaotic forecasting. It comes from a delay equation, sampled once per time unit; its quasi-period is about 50 steps:

dx/dt = 0.2 x(t−17) / (1 + x(t−17)¹⁰) − 0.1 x(t)

Splits are by time: train on the first 2,500 points, choose the best epoch (or λ) on the next 500, report the error on the last 1,000. The error is NRMSE: root-mean-square error divided by the standard deviation of the truth. 1 means "no better than predicting the mean", 0.01 means 1% of the signal's spread.

3.2 Experiment: Gradient-Based Training and Weight Dynamics

The task is the value 20 steps ahead. One step ahead is too easy: a plain linear model on the window already gets an NRMSE of 0.0006. Press Train. The heatmap is the first layer: one row per hidden unit, one column per position in the window. Every cell changes.

The window is all the memory it has. The Mackey–Glass equation feeds back x(t−17). Set L = 3 and train: the test NRMSE stays near 0.18 whatever the network does, because the information is not in its input. L = 10 gives about 0.017, L = 20 about 0.010. Press Reset to try another seed. The loss is non-convex, so each run ends somewhere slightly different.

4

Reservoir Computing: Fixed Random Recurrent Networks

Jaeger (2001, echo state networks) and Maass, Natschläger & Markram (2002, liquid state machines) proposed the same trick independently. Keep a big recurrent network, but don't train it: draw its weights at random, scale them so the network is stable, and let the input drive it. Learning only fits a linear readout.

4.1 State Equation, Hyperparameters and Readout

x(t) = (1−α) x(t−1) + α tanh(W_in u(t) + W x(t−1) + b)

Three numbers shape a reservoir:

  • the spectral radius ρ, the largest |λ| among the eigenvalues of W: how strongly the state echoes itself;
  • the leak rate α: how fast the state reacts (small α gives a slow, smooth state);
  • the input gain: how hard the input pushes the neurons into the nonlinear part of tanh.

Here each neuron listens to 10 random others. W is drawn once and rescaled to the chosen ρ. The page estimates ρ by power iteration, and tools/check.mjs compares that estimate with numpy's eigenvalues. Then only the readout is fitted:

W_out = Y Fᵀ (F Fᵀ + λnI)⁻¹, f(t) = [1; u(t); x(t)]

That is ridge regression: one linear solve with an (N+2)×(N+2) matrix. λ is chosen on the validation part, as in any regression.

4.2 Experiment: The Echo State Property

100 neurons, driven by the Mackey–Glass series. Two copies of the same reservoir start from different random states (runs A and B). The lower-right chart shows how far apart they are, on a log scale.

At the default settings the two runs merge after about 80 steps, down to machine precision. That is the echo state property. The reservoir forgets where it started, and its state becomes a function of the recent input history alone. This is why a readout trained on one stretch of data still works on another. It is also why training skips the first steps, the washout, when the state still depends on x(0).

"ρ < 1" is a rule of thumb, not the condition. At ρ = 1.2 the runs still merge (after about 180 steps); at ρ = 1.5 they do not within 300. Now set ρ = 2 and the input gain to 2. The strong input saturates the neurons, and the runs merge again after about 200 steps. Whether a reservoir has the echo state property depends on the reservoir and its input together.

4.3 Memory Capacity: Fading Memory without a Window

Drive the reservoir with white noise, and train one linear readout per delay k to reproduce u(t−k) from the state at time t. This is Jaeger's (2002) memory task. r² = 1 is perfect recall, 0 is none. A window of L values recalls exactly the delays it contains, and nothing beyond.

The reservoir's memory fades instead of stopping. With N = 100, ρ = 0.9, α = 1 and a small input gain, its memory capacity Σ r²(k) is about 30; theory caps it at N. Push ρ to 0.99 and it rises to about 34. Raise the input gain to 1 and the nonlinearity eats memory (about 12). Lower α to 0.3 and the state turns slow and smooth, so fast details are gone (about 11). The reservoir trades exact recall for a long, fading, nonlinear summary, and there is no L to choose.

Comparative Evaluation one task · one clock

Four models on one task: Mackey–Glass, 20 steps ahead, the same splits. The linear AR model, the ELM and the ESN are trained only at the readout, each in a single ridge solve. The FFN trains every weight for 150 epochs.

5.1 Experiment: Accuracy versus Training Time

Press Start. Each dot is a one-shot fit, placed at the moment it finished. The curve is the FFN's test error while it trains. The dashed lines mark the error the FFN would have to reach. Run a few seeds: the spread between them is the price of a non-convex loss.

5.2 Discussion

  • Readout-only training is fast, and here it is also accurate. The ESN and the ELM reach a test NRMSE of about 0.0004–0.0005 in about 0.1–0.2 s. The FFN needs about 2 s to reach about 0.010. Same data, same validation split.
  • The speed comes from the training, not from the recurrence. The ELM gets exactly the FFN's input, a window, and is about as accurate as the ESN. What makes it fast is that its hidden layer is random and fixed, which leaves a convex problem.
  • The recurrence buys memory without a window. Set L = 3. Every window model collapses (FFN ≈ 0.18, ELM ≈ 0.13, linear ≈ 0.60), while the ESN doesn't change: it never had a window.
  • Linear features are not enough. The linear AR model on the same window scores about 0.52. The accuracy comes from the many nonlinear random features.

So why isn't everything a reservoir? Gradient training scales to deep networks and large datasets. It learns its own features, where a random reservoir can only hope the right ones are among its random ones, and it handles inputs such as images or text, where a fixed random projection is a weak prior. Reservoirs shine on low-dimensional dynamical signals, on small data, and where training must be cheap or has to happen in physical hardware (optical, electronic or mechanical systems used as the reservoir).

Forecasting Real-World Time Series

A forecast is only as trustworthy as the evaluation around it. This part runs the same models on two real series from this repository and on Mackey–Glass, following the steps that keep a forecast honest.

6.1 Methodology

  1. Split by time: train, then validation, then test. Never shuffle across the boundaries; a forecast must not see the future.
  2. Scale with the training part only (its mean and standard deviation), and apply the same transform to the rest.
  3. Drop the reservoir's washout (the first 50 steps here), when its state still depends on x(0) = 0.
  4. Choose hyperparameters on validation (λ, the FFN's best epoch), then look at the test once.
  5. Beat the baselines: persistence ŷ(t+h) = z(t), and a seasonal naive forecast when the series has a season (the same day last week). A model that cannot beat them is not forecasting.
  6. Forecast every horizon at once (direct multi-step): one readout column, or one FFN output unit, per horizon h.
NRMSE_h = RMSE_h / std(y)

6.2 Experiment: Forecasting Benchmarks

Pick a series. The ESN, the linear AR model and the baselines are fitted right away; the FFN trains when you ask (a few seconds). The ρ and α sliders re-fit the ESN live. Move the starting point of the forecast through the test period.

What to look for:

  • Electricity demand. Every learned model beats both baselines: by a wide margin for tomorrow (NRMSE ≈ 0.34–0.38 against 0.70 for "same day last week") and by a little a week out (≈ 0.60 against 0.68). The FFN is slightly ahead, and the ESN ties the linear model. The largest errors fall on holidays, which none of these models knows about.
  • ECG. The ESN is best a few milliseconds ahead (0.08 against 0.20 for the FFN at 4 ms). Further out the two are close. Here the FFN is ahead at 100 ms (0.55 against 0.61); with other random draws, as in notebook 00, the ESN is. Neither predicts exactly when the next beat starts. That timing jitter (beat-to-beat intervals from 733 to 790 ms) is most of the error.
  • Mackey–Glass. A deterministic system is the reservoir's home ground. The ESN scores 0.010 at 50 steps ahead, the FFN 0.09.

Read the table, not just the chart. On noisy real data the reservoir is competitive, not magic. What it always wins is the fit time: tens to hundreds of milliseconds against seconds of training.

6.3 Closed-Loop (Autonomous) Forecasting

training
true u(t)→ model→ ŷ(t+1)compared with y(t+1)
free run
ŷ(t)→ model→ ŷ(t+1)becomes the next input

To forecast far ahead you can also train a one-step model and feed each prediction back as its next input. The model becomes an autonomous dynamical system that imitates the real one, and its errors compound. This is the setting that made reservoirs famous (Jaeger & Haas, Science, 2004).

Across seeds, the ESN stays within 0.2 standard deviations of the truth for about 540–860 steps (10–17 quasi-periods). The FFN, trained on the same one-step task with early stopping, lasts about 65–150 steps. Note how the ESN's λ is chosen: by the length of its valid free run on the validation part. The λ with the best one-step error is often too small to be stable once the model runs free.

7

Next-Generation Reservoir Computing (NG-RC)

Gauthier, Bollt, Griffith & Barbosa (2021) asked what the random reservoir actually contributes. Their next-generation reservoir computing drops it. The "state" is a few delayed inputs and their products: a nonlinear vector autoregression (NVAR). Most of the notebooks in this repository use this method.

7.1 Feature Construction: Time Delays and Polynomial Terms

O_lin = [v(t), v(t−s), …], O_total = c ⊕ O_lin ⊕ ⌈O_lin ⊗ O_lin⌉, v(t+1) = v(t) + W_out O_total

⌈⊗⌉ means the unique products (monomials). The readout predicts the step v(t+1) − v(t), like one step of a numerical integrator. It is ridge regression again, now on a few dozen chosen features instead of hundreds of random ones.

7.2 Dimensionality of the Feature Space

Count them for any number of channels d, taps k and degrees. The three examples are the configurations used by the notebooks in this repository.

The count grows fast. The cubic terms of 4 values already make 20; of 10 values, 220. That is why NG-RC suits low-dimensional systems.

7.3 Experiment: NG-RC versus ESN on the Lorenz-63 System

The Lorenz system (σ = 10, ρ = 28, β = 8/3, Δt = 0.025) is chaotic: nearby states move apart by a factor of e every Lyapunov time, 1/0.906 ≈ 1.1 time units, or 44 steps. Both models learn from the same points, then run free.

With 400 training points, NG-RC stays valid for about 4 Lyapunov times on average and the ESN for about 3.5. With 100 points the gap is 3 against 1. NG-RC needs a single past point to start (k − 1 = 1) instead of the ESN's 200-step washout, and has 84 trained weights instead of 912. Its advantage is data efficiency, not a higher ceiling. Given 1,000 points or more the ESN catches up and pulls ahead: about 5 and 6 Lyapunov times at 1,000 and 4,000 points, against 4.6 and 5.2 for NG-RC.

Two ablations to try. Untick quadratic: only linear features remain, and the forecast fails within 0.1 Lyapunov times. The nonlinearity does the work. Set k = 1: without a delay the model is like a single Euler step, and stays valid about one Lyapunov time. k = 2 works like a two-step integrator.

7.4 Position of NG-RC in the Model Taxonomy

Go back to the map in part 1. NG-RC has a window, like the FFN, and a fixed feature layer with a trained readout, like the ELM. It is the feed-forward cousin of the reservoir: a reservoir you can write down. It works so well on Lorenz partly because the Lorenz equations are themselves quadratic polynomials. A system whose equations look nothing like low-degree monomials needs many more features, or a real reservoir.

8

Automatic Control Theory and Reservoir Computing

The state-space model (A, B, C, D) is the basic language of control theory. A trained reservoir turns out to be a model of the same kind: random A and B, trained C and D.

8.1 State-Space Models

A linear time-invariant discrete-time system with one input u(t) and one output y(t) is specified by four matrices (A, B, C, D):

x(t) = A x(t−1) + B u(t), y(t) = C x(t) + D u(t)

Here x(t) ∈ ℝⁿ is the state vector, A the dynamics matrix, B the input matrix, C the output matrix and D the direct feedthrough. We use the same timing as the ESN (the state is updated with u(t) before the output is read); it differs from the standard form only by a one-step shift of the state. The input–output map is fully determined by the impulse response (the Markov parameters) and the transfer function:

h(0) = CB + D, h(k) = C Aᵏ B, G(z) = C (I − A z⁻¹)⁻¹ B + D

The plant is a second-order system with a pair of complex-conjugate poles z = r·e^{±iθ} in controllable canonical form; C is chosen so that the static gain (without D) equals 1. The radius r sets the damping (closer to 1 is slower), the angle θ the oscillation frequency in radians per step. The excitation is a random staircase, a standard test signal in system identification.

8.2 Experiment: Identifying the Plant with a Reservoir

A reservoir of 50 neurons receives the same input sequence u(t), and its readout is trained to reproduce the plant output y(t). This is a system identification problem: from a record of input and output, build a model that predicts the output for a new input. The bias b is zero and the input gain is small, so the neurons operate in the linear range of tanh. Training is one ridge solve; λ is chosen on the validation part and the model is scored on the test part.

With the default settings the test NRMSE is of order 10⁻³ or below. Three observations. First, with α = 1 the error rises to about 0.02: the eigenvalues of A fill a disk centred at zero, whereas the poles of a low-pass plant lie near z = 1. The leak shifts that disk towards 1 (section 1.3). Second, for a slow plant (r = 0.97, θ = 0.05) the error is about 0.18 at α = 0.5 and about 0.006 at α = 0.2: the eigenvalues of the reservoir must cover the region where the plant's poles lie. Third, with input gain 1 the neurons enter the nonlinear range of tanh and the error grows to 0.08: a linear plant is described less well by a nonlinear model.

8.3 The Trained Reservoir as an (A, B, C, D) Model

Linearize the reservoir equation at x = 0 with b = 0, where tanh′(0) = 1:

x(t) = [(1−α)I + αW] x(t−1) + α W_in u(t), ŷ(t) = W_out(x) x(t) + W_out(u) u(t) + w₀

The reservoir is therefore a state-space model in which A and B are drawn at random and fixed, and only C and D (and the constant w₀) are trained. The reservoir graph of section 2.3 is the signal-flow graph of A: vertices are state variables, edges are its nonzero entries, and the fans from the input and into the output are B and C. The eigenvalues of A equal 1 − α + α·λᵢ(W) and lie in a disk with centre 1 − α and radius αρ.

From the standpoint of control theory, several conclusions follow.

  • A non-minimal realization. The reservoir realizes a second-order transfer function with 50 states. The Hankel singular values σᵢ of the matrix [h(i+j+1)] reveal the true order of the map: for the trained reservoir, two significant values are followed by a drop of about three orders of magnitude. Model-order reduction (for example, balanced truncation) would recover a second-order model from it.
  • Non-uniqueness of the realization. A similarity transformation x → Tx leaves G(z) unchanged, and in a non-minimal realization C and D can offset each other. The trained D therefore differs from the plant's, while h(0) = CB + D agrees. The data determine the input–output map, not the matrices.
  • Relation to orthonormal basis function models. System identification has long used models whose poles are fixed in advance (Laguerre and Kautz bases) and whose output coefficients are found by least squares. A linear reservoir is a model of the same class with randomly chosen poles, so its accuracy depends on how well the eigenvalues of A cover the poles of the plant.
  • Stability. The linearized reservoir is asymptotically stable if all eigenvalues of A lie inside the unit circle. For a linear reservoir this is exactly the echo state property; for a nonlinear one the condition ρ < 1 is only a rule of thumb (section 4.2).
  • Limits of the interpretation. With a large input gain the reservoir is a nonlinear model x(t) = f(x(t−1), u(t)), and the (A, B, C, D) reading becomes local: A and B are the Jacobians of f at the operating point. Try input gain 1 in section 8.2: the impulse responses part ways.

8.4 Why the Plant's Poles and the Reservoir's Eigenvalues Can Be Compared

The z-plane in section 8.3 shows quantities of two different models: the poles of the plant (two values) and the eigenvalues of the reservoir's matrix A (50 values). Comparing them is legitimate for three reasons, but requires an important qualification.

  1. A common time scale. Both models are discrete-time with the same step. An eigenvalue λ contributes a term ρλᵏ to the impulse response, so a point of the z-plane stands for the same behaviour whichever model it belongs to:
    h(k) = Σ ρᵢ λᵢᵏ
  2. Invariance to the choice of coordinates. Under a change of state x → Tx the matrices become (TAT⁻¹, TB, CT⁻¹, D), while the eigenvalues of A do not change. As shown in 8.3, the matrices themselves are not determined by the data, whereas the eigenvalues are a coordinate-free property of the model. Models of different dimension (2 and 50 states) are therefore compared through them.
  3. Stability and memory. For both models |λ| < 1 means asymptotic stability, and the largest |λ| sets the slowest decay, that is, the length of the memory.

Qualification: the reservoir's eigenvalues are not estimates of the plant's poles. The poles of a transfer function coincide with the eigenvalues of A only for a minimal (controllable and observable) realization. The reservoir is a non-minimal realization, and its transfer function decomposes into modes:

G_res(z) = D + Σ ρᵢ z/(z − λᵢ)

Here vᵢ and wᵢ are the right and left eigenvectors. Training does not move the λᵢ: they are set by the random matrix W and the leak α. Only C is trained, and with it the residues ρᵢ. The plant's two modes are synthesized from many reservoir modes with large weights of opposite sign: with the default settings Σ|ρᵢ| ≈ 134 while max|h(k)| ≈ 0.2. The practical consequence is that a necessary condition for good identification is coverage: the reservoir must have eigenvalues near the plant's poles, in particular at least as slow (|λᵢ| ≥ |p|); otherwise no combination of them can reproduce the slow tail pᵏ. This is what the slow-plant experiment of 8.2 showed: lowering α moves the eigenvalues towards z = 1, and the error falls from 0.18 to 0.006.

How to estimate the poles from the reservoir. The proper procedure is to reduce the reservoir model to a minimal one and compare the poles of that model. The Ho–Kalman algorithm (the eigensystem realization algorithm, ERA) builds Hankel matrices from the impulse response, keeps their two principal directions and recovers a second-order dynamics matrix:

H₀ ≈ PQ, A_r = P⁺ H₁ Q⁺

The poles of A_r are shown as rings on the z-plane in 8.3. With the default settings they coincide with the plant's poles to about 10⁻⁴. With input gain 1 they part (0.879 ± 0.308i against 0.860 ± 0.266i): the reservoir operates in its nonlinear range, and its linearization no longer describes the input–output map.

Conclusions

No family wins everywhere. Each wins where its assumptions hold.
